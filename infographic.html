The Hidden Cost of "Next-Day" Delivery

The button says "Get it tomorrow." The bill shows up somewhere else: in a warehouse, on a city street, in a pile of returns. Pick a tab to see where.

Speed gets paid for in bodies

31%

more injuries at Amazon warehouses than the warehousing industry average in 2023.

~2×

as likely to be injured as workers at non-Amazon warehouses, in each of the past seven years.

A Senate committee spent 18 months on this and concluded that pressure to hit speed targets is a major driver of the injuries.

Fair warning: Amazon disputes the report. It says it does not under-report injuries and stands by how it compares its numbers. I use Amazon as a case study because it is the largest player, not because it is the only one.

More vans, more traffic, more CO₂

The World Economic Forum projects 36% more delivery vehicles in the world's top 100 cities by 2030. Toggle between two futures. The dashed line is today (= 100).

Do nothing: emissions +32%, congestion +21%.

The surprise: the "fixed" future still only lands near today's emissions. Those fixes (like consolidating deliveries and cleaner vehicles) cut 30% from the do-nothing path, so 132 × 0.70 ≈ 92. That index is my own calculation from the WEF numbers.

Every return is another trip, another box, another handling step. Retailers surveyed list "reducing return rates" as a top 2026 priority.

What can one person do?

  • Choose the slower shipping option when you don't need it tomorrow.
  • Bundle orders instead of sending five separate boxes.
  • Skip "bracketing" (ordering three sizes to keep one).
  • Ask which cost you're really paying: money is the only one the checkout page shows.
